# Why the brain replays the day

During the day the hippocampus records where you went and what happened there. A rat running a maze fires a chain of place cells, one after another, as it moves along the track. The chain is a compressed transcript of the route.

At night the transcript plays again. In slow-wave sleep the same cells fire in the same order, only faster, packed into bursts a few hundred milliseconds long. This replay is thought to move fragile memories into the cortex, where they can survive without the hippocampus.

The evidence started with recordings in sleeping rats (Wilson and McNaughton, 1994), and it has since been seen in birds, monkeys and, more indirectly, in people. The open questions are about mechanism: what triggers replay, what decides which experiences get replayed, and what the cortex does with the copy it receives.

Three ideas organise the field. Replay is a form of rehearsal. Replay is a form of transfer, from a fast learner to a slow one. And replay is a form of editing, in which the day's events are recombined into something the animal never experienced.
