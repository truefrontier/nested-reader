# Sharp-wave ripples

A sharp-wave ripple is a brief burst of high-frequency activity in the hippocampus, lasting about a tenth of a second. It is the container in which replay happens: the same sequence of place cells that fired along a route fires again, compressed roughly twenty-fold.

Ripples cluster during slow-wave sleep and quiet wakefulness. Disrupting them selectively, without otherwise waking the animal, impairs memory for the route learned that day, which is the strongest causal evidence that replay is doing the consolidating rather than merely reflecting it.

Ripples are also where the day's route is most often observed to replay in the [[same-order|same order]] it was run, though reverse replay appears at reward sites.

Frequency sits between 150 and 250 Hz in rodents, lower in primates. Whether human ripples serve the same role is inferred mostly from intracranial recordings in epilepsy patients, where ripple rate before recall predicts success.
