Sharp-wave ripples

A sharp-wave ripple is a brief burst of high-frequency activity in the hippocampus, lasting about a tenth of a second. It is the container in which replay happens: the same sequence of place cells that fired along a route fires again, compressed roughly twenty-fold.

Ripples cluster during slow-wave sleep and quiet wakefulness. Selective disruption means firing a short electrical pulse the moment a ripple is detected, aborting it. Animals stay asleep, sleep architecture is unchanged, yet next-day recall of the route drops to near the level of rats that never slept.

Ripples are also where the day's route is most often observed to replay in the same order it was run, though reverse replay appears at reward sites.

Frequency sits between 150 and 250 Hz in rodents, lower in primates. Whether human ripples serve the same role is inferred mostly from intracranial recordings in epilepsy patients, where ripple rate before recall predicts success.

Why does disrupting a ripple erase the route?

Because the ripple is the only window in which the hippocampal sequence is broadcast to cortex. Cut it short and the cortex receives a fragment, not enough to strengthen the distributed trace that later recall depends on.

The effect is specific: disrupting ripples that occur outside the post-learning window does nothing, and jittering the pulse by a few hundred milliseconds so it misses the ripple also does nothing.
